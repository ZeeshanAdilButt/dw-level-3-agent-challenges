# Redwater Legal: customer story

Redwater Legal is a mid-sized commercial law practice that advises businesses on supply agreements, employment contracts, property leases, and financing arrangements. Its lawyers and paralegals handle a steady stream of documents, and for most of the firm's history those documents lived in a patchwork of shared drives, email attachments, and a filing system that only the longest-serving staff fully understood. This story describes how the firm moved its contract work onto Quillon, what it configured, and what changed as a result. It is published with the firm's permission and reflects its own account of the project.

## The problem before Quillon

The firm's difficulties were not unusual, but they were getting more expensive. Drafting a new agreement often meant opening an old one, copying the parts that still applied, and hoping nothing stale came along with them. Clauses were rewritten from memory across teams, so two lawyers advising similar clients could end up with materially different language on the same point. Approvals happened by email, which made it hard to show who had signed off on what, and harder still to find that record months later during a client review.

Obligations were the quietest risk. A supply agreement might require notice before a price review, or a lease might carry a break clause with a short window, and these dates were tracked in individual lawyers' calendars rather than anywhere the wider team could see. When a responsible lawyer was out of the office, the date could pass unnoticed. Renewals had a similar weakness: contracts rolled over automatically because nobody remembered that a decision was due.

Leadership at Redwater Legal was clear that the firm did not want to add a tool for its own sake. The goal was to make the existing work more consistent and to reduce the number of things that depended on one person's memory.

## What Redwater Legal set up

The rollout was planned in phases. The first phase moved the firm's core templates into Quillon's drafting environment. The firm's practice leads reviewed each template, removed out-of-date language, and marked the approved versions. Drafting from these templates means a new agreement starts from a known position rather than from a copy of a copy.

The second phase built out the clause library. Lawyers were asked to nominate the clauses they used most often on indemnity, limitation of liability, termination, data handling, and payment terms, and the practice leads agreed a standard wording for each one along with a short note on when a deviation was acceptable and who needed to approve it. Clauses in the library are versioned, so when the firm updates its position on a point, the change is visible and the earlier wording remains on record for documents already signed.

The third phase was approvals. Redwater Legal configured its review process as 40 approval chains, each tied to a type of matter or a level of risk. A low-value renewal on standard terms follows a short chain with one reviewer. A non-standard indemnity for a larger client follows a longer chain that includes a senior partner. Each step is recorded, so the firm can show at any point who reviewed a document, what they were asked to approve, and when they did so.

Signature and storage were the final pieces. Execution happens through Quillon's e-signature function, and signed agreements are held in the same repository as the drafts that produced them. The firm then turned on obligation tracking and renewal alerts, so that notice periods, review dates, and renewal windows are entered once and surfaced to the people responsible, with reminders that run ahead of each deadline. Search across signed contracts meant that a question such as which agreements contain a particular data handling commitment could be answered in minutes rather than by reading through folders.

Training was kept deliberately short. The firm ran sessions for each practice group, focused on the workflows people would use every day, and it appointed one lawyer in each group as the first point of contact for questions. Redwater Legal's view was that adoption depended less on the training itself than on whether the templates and clause library were good enough that people wanted to use them.

The rollout reached 190 users across 7 jurisdictions. The firm's administrators reported that most of the difficulty came in the early weeks, when older matters had to be migrated and some legacy documents needed tidying before they could be searched reliably. Once that work was done, the day-to-day usage settled quickly.

## What the firm says has changed

Kofi Mensah, a senior associate who leads the firm's commercial contracts group, described the change in practical terms. "The biggest difference is that I no longer start a draft by hunting for the last version I used," he said. "I start from the approved template, I pull the clauses I need from the library, and when someone needs to approve a deviation, they can see exactly what I'm proposing and why. The renewal alerts alone have saved us from missing windows that we would previously have only caught by luck."

The firm reports two measured outcomes from the first year of use. The first concerns review consistency: across the commercial matters Redwater Legal tracked, the proportion of agreements drafted from approved templates rose sharply after the rollout, and the number of non-standard clauses requiring escalation fell. The second concerns the approval record. Because every review now sits inside one of its approval chains, the firm can answer audit questions about any document from its own system rather than reconstructing the history from email.

Redwater Legal also noted an effect that is harder to quantify. Newer lawyers can now see how the firm has handled a particular kind of clause over time, which the firm believes shortens the period before they can work independently on routine agreements. The firm does not put a number on this, but its practice leads describe it as one of the more welcome changes in day-to-day work.

## Looking ahead

Redwater Legal is now extending its clause library to cover the regulatory language that varies between its jurisdictions, and it plans to review its approval chains annually as the firm's practice areas evolve. The firm's leadership expects the repository of signed agreements to become a more valuable asset as the volume grows, since search across past contracts is only as useful as the contracts it covers.

Redwater Legal's experience reflects a simple principle that the firm applies to its own clients: good contract management is mostly about making the right information available at the right moment, to the right people. The firm says the platform has made that easier, and it is continuing to build on the foundations laid during the rollout.
