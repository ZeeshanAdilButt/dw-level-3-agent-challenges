# Fenwick Capital Partners: customer story

## The challenge before Quillon

Fenwick Capital Partners is a mid-sized investment firm whose finance and legal teams share responsibility for a large body of commercial agreements. These include investor side letters, vendor service contracts, lease schedules for shared offices, and financing documents tied to the funds the firm manages. Before adopting Quillon, the firm kept most of this material in a mix of shared drives, email attachments, and a spreadsheet that one senior analyst maintained by hand. Finding the current version of an agreement often meant asking a colleague who might remember where it was filed, and confirming that a signed copy existed could take most of an afternoon.

The difficulty was not only retrieval. Renewal dates were tracked in calendar reminders that individuals set for themselves, and obligations such as notice periods, reporting deadlines, and insurance certificate renewals lived in the heads of whoever negotiated each deal. When an auditor asked for evidence that a particular obligation had been met, the finance team had to reconstruct the answer from correspondence. Each audit cycle became a project in its own right, pulling people away from analysis and into document hunts.

Leadership had also become concerned about drafting consistency. Similar agreements were being written from whichever precedent a lawyer happened to have open, which meant clauses on confidentiality, limitation of liability, and governing law varied from one document to the next without a deliberate reason. The firm wanted a single place where approved language lived, where drafts were produced from that language, and where every signed document could be searched by its terms as well as its title.

## Why Fenwick chose Quillon

Fenwick evaluated several contract-management products over a period of several weeks. The team was looking for three things: a drafting workflow that started from approved templates, a clause library that the legal group could maintain without developer help, and a repository that could hold a large historical archive without a costly migration. Quillon's combination of template-based drafting, approval routing, electronic signature, and obligation tracking in one system matched the scope of what the firm needed, so the firm did not have to stitch together separate tools for each stage.

The Enterprise plan was selected because the finance team needed controls over who could edit approved clauses, detailed audit logging, and the ability to run cross-document searches across the full archive. The firm's operations lead described the decision as a practical one: the team wanted to move the whole lifecycle into a single system rather than manage exceptions between several.

## What Fenwick set up

The implementation began with an inventory of the existing agreements. Rather than attempting to import everything at once, the team prioritized active contracts and those with upcoming renewal or notice dates, then brought in the historical archive in batches. Each imported document was checked against its signed original, and key metadata such as counterparty, effective date, term, and governing law was captured in structured fields so that the search and alert features would work reliably.

Next, Fenwick's legal group built a clause library. Working with outside counsel, they identified the language the firm was willing to accept on its most common provisions and marked a set of approved alternatives for each. Drafting templates were then created for the five agreement types the firm uses most often, each pulling from the library so that a new document started from language the firm had already agreed to. Approval routing was configured so that deviations from standard clauses required sign-off from a named reviewer before a draft could go to a counterparty.

Obligation tracking was the final layer. The team defined the recurring and one-time obligations in each active agreement, such as reporting deliverables, insurance certificates, and notice windows for renewal, and assigned each to an owner. Renewal alerts were set to notify the responsible person well ahead of each deadline, with a summary sent to the finance lead. The e-signature workflow was connected so that executed copies flowed back into the repository automatically, removing the step of manually filing signed PDFs.

## Staff perspective

Rachel Osei, Head of Finance Operations at Fenwick, led the rollout and described the change in terms of day-to-day work. "Our audit cycle used to be the part of the year everyone dreaded," she said. "Now the evidence is already attached to the agreement it relates to, and I can answer the auditor's question in the time it takes to run a search. The clause library also changed how our lawyers work. They are not starting from a blank page or a document from three years ago anymore."

She also noted that adoption depended on making the system the path of least resistance. The team ran short working sessions for each group that would use the platform, and the legal group kept a single owner for the clause library so that questions about approved language had a clear answer.

## Results

Fenwick's measured results focus on the audit process and the reliability of the archive. The most significant change is in preparation time: the firm cut audit preparation time by 60%, a reduction the finance team attributes mainly to documents being searchable and linked to their obligations rather than assembled by hand. Staff who previously spent days locating evidence now assemble the same package from within the platform.

Rollout also changed how the firm handles the archive itself. Fenwick now stores 31,000 signed contracts in Quillon, covering both current agreements and the historical record that had previously been scattered across storage locations. Because each one is indexed by its terms and obligations, the firm can answer questions such as which agreements contain a particular notice provision or which counterparties have upcoming renewal dates without opening individual files.

## Looking ahead

Fenwick plans to extend obligation tracking to its portfolio company agreements over the coming year and to expand the clause library to cover additional jurisdictions. The team has also asked for more granular reporting on approval turnaround times, which it intends to use to identify bottlenecks in the drafting process.

Rachel Osei summed up the outcome simply: the firm no longer treats contract records as a separate chore from the work itself. The agreements, the language behind them, and the obligations they create now live in one place, and the people responsible for them can see what they need to know when they need to know it.
