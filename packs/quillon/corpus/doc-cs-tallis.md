# Tallis Barrett LLP: customer story

## Overview

Tallis Barrett LLP is a commercial law firm that advises mid-sized companies on supply agreements, employment contracts, software licensing and real estate leases. Like many firms of its size, it handles a steady stream of contracts that follow familiar patterns but still require careful reading. This customer story describes how the firm moved its contract work onto Quillon's contract-management platform, what changed in day-to-day practice, and what the firm measured after the rollout. It is published with the firm's permission and reflects its own account of the project.

The firm operates on Quillon's Pro plan, which it uses across its corporate, commercial and employment groups. The platform covers drafting from templates, a shared clause library, approval routing, electronic signature, obligation tracking, renewal alerts and search across the firm's signed contracts.

## The problem before Quillon

Before the rollout, contract work at Tallis Barrett was spread across shared drives, email threads and individual lawyers' local folders. Templates existed, but they were often copied, edited and saved under new names, so the firm rarely knew which version of a clause was the current approved wording. A partner asking whether the firm had ever accepted a particular limitation-of-liability position would often get a different answer depending on whom they asked.

The first review of an incoming counterparty contract was the most time-consuming stage. A junior lawyer would read the full document, compare each clause against the firm's preferred positions from memory or from a reference memo, and flag deviations for a senior colleague. This work was necessary, but much of it was repetitive, and the firm's senior lawyers were frequently pulled into first-pass review when their time was better spent on the negotiation itself.

Obligation tracking was another weak point. Notice periods, reporting deadlines and renewal dates lived in spreadsheets that someone had to remember to update. Missed renewal windows were uncommon but costly, and the firm's risk team wanted a more reliable system.

## What Tallis Barrett set up

The project was led by the firm's operations lead, with support from a small working group of partners and associates. The group began by consolidating the firm's templates into a single library inside Quillon, retiring duplicate versions and assigning an owner to each template family. This step took longer than expected because several practice groups had built their own variants over time, and each variant had to be compared and reconciled before it could be retired.

Next, the working group built out the clause library. Approved clauses were stored with notes on when each variant should be used and which fallback positions the firm was willing to accept. Lawyers could insert clauses directly into drafts from the library, which reduced the number of places where outdated wording could creep in.

The firm then turned on AI clause review for incoming counterparty paper. When a contract is uploaded, the tool identifies the clauses present, compares them against the approved library, and highlights deviations with a short explanation of how each differs from the firm's standard position. The reviewing lawyer still makes every judgment call. The tool's role is to direct attention, so that the lawyer starts from a marked-up view of the document rather than from a blank reading of every page.

Approval routing was configured by matter type and by deal value, so that a deviation above a certain threshold automatically reached a senior partner before the redline went back to the counterparty. Electronic signature was enabled for the firm's standard agreements, and signed documents were filed automatically into the searchable repository. Obligation tracking and renewal alerts were set up for each signed contract, with the notice and renewal dates taken from the executed agreement and reviewed by the matter lead at signing.

## Rollout

The rollout ran in stages. The corporate group went first, because its contract volume was highest and its templates were the most mature. The employment group followed, and the commercial real estate team joined last. Each group had a named internal champion who ran short working sessions, collected feedback and maintained a running list of questions for the Quillon onboarding team.

Early adoption was uneven. Some lawyers were initially sceptical of the clause review output, particularly where the tool flagged a deviation that the lawyer considered immaterial in context. The working group responded by adjusting how the library notes were written, so that each approved clause explained its acceptable range more clearly, and by encouraging lawyers to record when they disagreed with a flag. Over the following months, the disagreement notes became a useful source of feedback for refining the library itself.

Training was kept practical. Rather than lengthy manuals, the firm produced a one-page guide for each matter type and recorded short walkthroughs of the most common tasks: starting a draft from a template, inserting a clause, routing an approval and sending a document for signature. New joiners were added to the platform as part of their onboarding, and the operations team reviewed usage monthly during the first year.

Partners were involved from the start. The firm's managing partner asked that every practice group contribute to the clause library, which helped avoid the impression that the system was being imposed on one team. That decision, according to the operations lead, did more to build trust than any individual feature.

## Results

The firm tracked a small set of measures before and after the rollout, focusing on the stages where it expected the most change. The most significant result was in first-pass review. Tallis Barrett cut first-pass review time by 52% with AI clause review, measured across incoming counterparty contracts over the period following the commercial rollout. The firm reports that the reduction came mainly from fewer hours spent locating clauses and comparing them against precedent, rather than from any reduction in the depth of the lawyer's own reading.

The firm also reports that the template and clause consolidation produced less tangible but still valued gains. Partners can now answer questions about past positions by searching signed contracts rather than by reconstructing history from memory. Obligation tracking has reduced the number of renewal windows that come close to passing unnoticed, and the risk team now reviews a single consolidated list rather than several spreadsheets.

Volume is the context for these figures. Tallis Barrett LLP reviews 300 contracts a month, across all of its practice groups, so even modest per-document savings add up to a meaningful change in capacity.

## Quote from the firm

"The biggest change is that our senior lawyers now start a review with a clear map of where a contract departs from our positions, instead of spending their first hour finding the clauses that matter," said the firm's operations lead. "We still make every judgment ourselves. What's changed is where our time goes, and that means more of it goes to the negotiation, which is where clients need us most."

## Looking ahead

Tallis Barrett plans to extend the clause library to cover the remaining practice areas and to review its approval thresholds after the first full year of operation. The firm is also considering using search across signed contracts more systematically in its client reporting, so that clients can see the status of their agreements in one place.
