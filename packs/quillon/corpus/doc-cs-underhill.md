# Underhill Capital: customer story

## Background

Underhill Capital is a corporate finance team that manages a steady flow of commercial agreements, including lending terms, vendor contracts, equipment leases, and side letters with portfolio partners. The team is small relative to the volume it handles, and most of its members split their time between deal work and compliance tasks. Contracts have historically lived across shared drives, email attachments, and a spreadsheet that was meant to track key dates but was rarely current.

The team is on Quillon's Enterprise plan, which gives them access to the full contract-management suite: drafting from templates, a shared clause library, multi-step approvals, built-in e-signature, obligation tracking, renewal alerts, and search across all signed contracts. This story describes how they got there, what they changed, and what they measured.

## The problem before Quillon

Before adopting Quillon, the finance team's contract process depended heavily on individual memory. A senior analyst would know that a certain covenant required a quarterly certificate, but if that analyst was out, the reminder might not reach anyone. Renewal dates were tracked in a spreadsheet that had grown over several years, with columns that no longer matched the contracts they described. Drafting meant copying a previous agreement, deleting the parts that did not apply, and hoping the remaining language was still correct.

Approvals ran over email. A draft would circulate with a chain of replies attached, and it was often unclear which version a reviewer had actually read. Signed copies were filed by counterparty name in folders that different people organized differently. When the internal audit group asked for evidence that specific obligations had been met, the team spent considerable time reconstructing the record from correspondence.

These problems did not produce a single dramatic failure. They produced a steady stream of small gaps, and those gaps accumulated into audit findings. The team's leadership decided that the process needed structure rather than more diligence from individuals.

## What the team set up

The implementation began with a decision about scope. Rather than migrating every historical contract at once, the team chose to bring in active agreements first, then work backward through older documents as time allowed. Each active contract was entered into Quillon with its key terms extracted: parties, effective dates, renewal windows, notice periods, and a list of recurring or one-time obligations.

The team then built a clause library. Their most-used provisions, including confidentiality language, indemnification terms, governing law clauses, and payment schedules, were stored once, approved by legal counsel, and tagged by the circumstances in which they applied. Drafting from templates now meant selecting a base document and then choosing approved clauses from the library, which reduced the number of bespoke sentences that needed review.

Approval routing was configured to match the team's actual authority structure. A draft over a certain value went to a senior reviewer and then to the compliance group, while routine renewals followed a shorter path. Because every step was recorded, the team could see exactly which version each approver had reviewed and when they had acted.

E-signature was enabled for counterparties who were comfortable with it, and signed documents were filed automatically with their associated obligations. Obligation tracking was the piece that changed daily habits most. Each recurring requirement, such as a delivery of financial statements or a periodic certification, became a tracked item with an owner and a due date. Renewal alerts were set to notify the responsible person well before each notice window closed.

The team also used the sandbox environment to test new workflows before putting them in front of the whole group. Over the course of the rollout, Underhill Capital used the sandbox to test 5 new workflows, including a revised approval path for amendments, a template for side letters, and an obligation-reminder structure for multi-party agreements. Testing in the sandbox allowed the team to catch configuration errors, such as a routing rule that skipped a required reviewer, before they affected live contracts.

## The rollout

The rollout was deliberately gradual. The first two weeks focused on the active contract set and on training the core group who would administer the system. The team held short working sessions in which each member entered a contract they knew well and then used search to find it again, which surfaced naming conventions that needed to be agreed on early.

The second phase brought in the broader finance and legal staff. Some members were initially skeptical, particularly those who had built personal systems over many years. The team addressed this by keeping the spreadsheet available in read-only form for a transition period, and by showing people that search across signed contracts could answer questions that previously required asking a colleague.

The third phase concerned discipline rather than setup. The team agreed that no contract would be considered current until it was entered in Quillon with its obligations tracked, and that drafts would move through the approval workflow rather than through email. Compliance reviews now begin with a report from the obligation tracker, not with a request to colleagues for recollections.

Throughout the rollout, the administrator maintained a short log of issues and resolutions. Most issues were minor, such as a template field that needed a clearer label or a reminder that fired to a former team member. Each was corrected within the working week, and the log became a useful reference for onboarding later hires.

## What changed

The most visible change was in audit preparation. Where the team once spent days assembling evidence, they now produce it from the system. Contract-related findings in the most recent audit cycle fell from 9 to 1, and the team attributes much of that improvement to having obligations tracked with owners and dates, along with a complete approval record for each agreement.

Day-to-day work also shifted. Renewal decisions are made in advance because alerts arrive with enough lead time to negotiate. Drafting is faster because the clause library reduces the number of provisions that need fresh review. Search across signed contracts has replaced a good deal of informal asking around, and new team members can find precedent language without relying on a senior colleague's memory.

## Quote from the team

Priya Raman, Head of Finance Operations at Underhill Capital, described the change this way:

"We used to know our contracts through the people who had worked on them. Now the contracts can tell us what they require, when those requirements fall due, and who is responsible. That has taken a lot of pressure off individuals, and it has made our audit conversations much shorter."

## Looking ahead

The team plans to continue migrating older agreements into Quillon over the coming year, prioritizing those with ongoing obligations. They are also exploring how the clause library can be extended to cover side letters and amendments more consistently. Their administrator has begun documenting the configuration choices they made so that future team members can understand why approval routes and reminder schedules are set as they are.

Underhill Capital's experience reflects the team's own priorities and working style. Other finance groups will have different structures and different starting points. What the team found valuable was less the software itself than the discipline of recording obligations, approvals, and signed records in one place, and of testing changes before rolling them out widely.
