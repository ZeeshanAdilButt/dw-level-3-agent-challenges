# Marlow Industrial Finance: Customer Story

Marlow Industrial Finance is a commercial lending and equipment finance company whose corporate finance team manages a large portfolio of supplier, service, and technology agreements. For years, those agreements lived in a mix of shared drives, email attachments, and spreadsheets maintained by different people for different purposes. This customer story describes how the team moved its contract operations onto Quillon, what problems it set out to solve, how the rollout went, and what the team has measured since.

The team is on Quillon's Enterprise plan. Its members work with the legal department on drafting and approvals, but the day-to-day responsibility for vendor relationships sits with finance. That split meant that the people who negotiated a contract were often not the people who tracked its deadlines, and the people who tracked deadlines were often working from a copy that was out of date.

## The problem before Quillon

Before adopting Quillon, the finance team had no single place to find the current version of any agreement. A signed contract might exist as a scanned PDF in one folder, a redlined Word file in another, and a line in a spreadsheet that said only "renews in the fall." When a vendor relationship needed review, someone had to ask around to determine which document governed the arrangement.

Renewal dates were the most costly weakness. Many of the company's vendor agreements contained automatic renewal clauses with notice windows that were easy to miss. Notice periods varied from contract to contract, and nobody had a reliable, consolidated view of when each window opened and closed. The result was a pattern of renewals that happened by default, sometimes on terms the company would have renegotiated had it known the deadline was approaching.

Obligations inside the contracts were tracked even less systematically. Service levels, reporting requirements, insurance certificates, and pricing review dates were recorded, if at all, in the memory of the person who had originally handled the deal. When that person changed roles or left the company, the knowledge went with them.

## What the team set up

The finance team began its Quillon rollout by importing its existing signed agreements into the platform's contract repository. This was the tedious part. The team sorted documents by vendor, confirmed which version was the executed copy, and attached supporting amendments and side letters to the correct parent agreement. Because Quillon's search works across signed contracts, the team could begin testing its ability to find terms, counterparties, and clauses as soon as the first batch was loaded.

Next, the team built its standard templates in the platform's drafting tool and populated the clause library with approved language for the provisions it negotiates most often, including termination rights, limitation of liability, data handling, and renewal mechanics. Drafting from templates meant that new agreements started from language the legal department had already reviewed, rather than from whatever the last counterparty had sent over.

The team then configured its approval workflows so that new agreements and material amendments moved through the right reviewers in a fixed order before reaching e-signature. Every signature request and every approval was recorded against the contract, which gave the team an audit trail it had previously assembled by hand.

The most important configuration was obligation tracking and renewal alerts. For each active agreement, the team entered its key dates and obligations, including the notice window for each automatic renewal. Quillon then generated alerts ahead of those windows, routed to the finance owner for each vendor. The team's goal was that no renewal would again arrive as a surprise.

## Rollout

The rollout was staged rather than sudden. In the first phase, a small group of finance staff used the platform for new agreements only, while the existing portfolio remained in the old folders. This allowed the team to refine its templates and approval routing without disrupting work already in progress.

In the second phase, the team migrated the legacy portfolio in groups organized by renewal date, starting with the agreements whose notice windows were closest. This sequencing was deliberate. Working through the nearest deadlines first meant the team could act on real renewals while the rest of the migration continued. Several agreements surfaced during this phase needed immediate attention, and the team was able to open renegotiation discussions in time.

In the third phase, the team retired its shared spreadsheet of contract dates and made Quillon the single source for contract status. Training focused less on the software's features and more on a simple habit: if a question about a vendor agreement comes up, the answer is found in the platform, and any change to an agreement is recorded there.

Adoption was not without friction. Some staff initially preferred to keep personal copies of documents, and the team addressed this by making the platform the only path to the executed version. The team also found that the quality of the migrated data mattered a great deal. Entries with incorrect renewal dates had to be corrected before the alerts could be trusted, and the team made that verification a formal step in the migration.

## Measured results

The team has measured two outcomes since its rollout. First, the review of its renewal calendar identified $1.4 million in missed auto-renewal savings, which were recovered by renegotiating or exiting agreements before their notice windows closed. These were savings that had previously renewed unnoticed.

Second, the team now monitors 3,900 vendor contracts through Quillon's obligation tracking and renewal alerts. Every one of those agreements has a named owner, recorded key dates, and tracked obligations, so the team can see its full vendor portfolio in one place rather than reconstructing it on request.

## What the team says

A senior member of the finance team described the change in practical terms. "We used to find out about a renewal after it had already happened," the team lead said. "Now the alert arrives early enough that we can decide whether we want the same terms, and in most cases we have a real choice to make. The bigger change is that when someone asks what we agreed to with a vendor, we can answer in minutes instead of days."

The team continues to expand its use of the platform, with its next priorities centered on tightening the link between obligation tracking and the vendor reviews that take place each quarter.
