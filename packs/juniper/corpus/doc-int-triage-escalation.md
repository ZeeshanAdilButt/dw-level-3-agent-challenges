# Triage escalation protocol

**Juniper Lane Clinic, Port Avery**
**Document type:** Internal playbook
**Audience:** Internal (clinical and reception staff only, not for patients)
**Authority:** Rule
**Effective from:** 1 September 2026

## 1. Purpose and scope

This protocol sets out how calls and walk-in enquiries that show signs of a serious or time-sensitive condition are triaged, handed over and escalated within Juniper Lane Clinic. It applies to every member of staff who answers the main line, the telehealth request queue, the reception desk, or a clinician's internal messages during clinic hours. It does not replace clinical judgement. Where this protocol and a clinician's direct instruction conflict, follow the clinician and record the reason.

The clinic offers family medicine, child health checks, vaccinations and a travel clinic, blood tests, physiotherapy, women's health, minor procedures, repeat prescription administration and telehealth calls. Triage escalation applies across all of these services.

## 2. Definitions

A **red flag call** is any contact in which the caller describes symptoms or circumstances that could indicate a life-threatening or rapidly deteriorating condition. Examples include difficulty breathing, chest pain, a sudden change in consciousness, heavy bleeding, signs of stroke, or a child who is hard to rouse. When in doubt, treat the call as a red flag.

An **urgent hand-off** is the transfer of a patient contact from a receptionist or call handler to a clinician, made because the matter cannot wait for the next routine appointment.

The **duty clinician** is the clinician rostered to cover urgent contacts at the time the call is taken. The duty roster is held at reception and in the shared staff calendar.

The **clinical lead** is the senior clinician responsible for clinical governance on any given day.

## 3. Recognising a red flag

Staff should not attempt to diagnose. Ask the standard screening questions from the reception script, record the answers verbatim in the contact record, and move to escalation as soon as any answer matches a red flag. Do not wait for the caller to confirm severity. A caller who sounds calm but describes a red flag feature still counts as a red flag call.

## 4. Escalation steps

Follow these steps in order.

1. Stay on the line and keep the caller engaged. Do not end the call to make the hand-off.
2. Confirm the caller's name, date of birth and callback number, and the location of the patient if different from the caller.
3. Classify the call as a red flag call. Note the specific feature that triggered the classification.
4. Put the call straight through to the duty clinician. Red flag calls go straight to the duty clinician and Dr Elsworth is paged at once, without first checking availability or waiting for a reply.
5. Page Dr Elsworth at once using the internal paging system, referencing the call time and the feature noted in step 3.
6. If the duty clinician is not immediately available, keep the caller on the line and use the hand-off script to give the clinician a verbal summary as soon as they pick up.
7. A caller describing a red flag is never put on hold for more than 30 seconds. If a transfer cannot be completed within that time, return to the caller, give them a clear instruction to call emergency services if their symptoms worsen, and continue trying the transfer.
8. For an urgent hand-off that is not a red flag but still needs same-day clinician contact, send it to the duty clinician's queue and mark it urgent. An urgent hand-off must be picked up within 5 minutes.
9. Record each step with a time stamp in the contact record, including who answered the hand-off and when.

## 5. When a hand-off is not picked up

If nobody picks up an urgent hand-off in 5 minutes, the clinical lead is paged on extension 7. The sender remains responsible for the contact until a clinician has accepted it. Do not close the record or tell the caller the matter is resolved until acceptance is confirmed.

If the clinical lead also does not respond within a further short interval, the receptionist on duty should inform the office manager, who will determine whether any clinician is physically available on site or whether the caller should be directed to an emergency service.

## 6. Telehealth and out-of-hours contacts

Telehealth requests are triaged using the same red flag criteria. If a telehealth request raises a red flag during booking, the booking must not be confirmed. Escalate as set out in section 4 and advise the caller, through the approved script, to contact emergency services if they are in immediate danger.

Repeat prescription requests that raise a clinical concern, such as a reported adverse reaction or a missed dose of a critical medicine, should be treated as urgent hand-offs even where no red flag is described.

## 7. Documentation and review

Every red flag call and every urgent hand-off must have a complete contact record. Records are reviewed weekly by the clinical lead for timeliness of pick-up and adherence to the 30 second hold limit and the 5 minute pick-up standard. Repeated delays are reported at the monthly governance meeting.

Staff who are unsure whether a contact meets the threshold should escalate rather than hold it. An unnecessary escalation is always preferable to a delayed one.

## 8. Training and version control

All reception and clinical staff must complete triage escalation training before taking calls unsupervised, and refresh it annually. This protocol takes effect on 1 September 2026 and supersedes any earlier informal escalation practice.
